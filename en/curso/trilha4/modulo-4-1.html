⌨️ How to invoke

There are two ways to trigger grill-me, and both work: the slash command (/grill-me) e a natural language. How the skill loads through your description, just describe what you want and it kicks in automatically.

Slash command? It’s the shortcut /nome-do-skill that invokes a skill directly in Claude Code. The alternative is simply to write the request in English — the skill is triggered by its description.

Example to run— invoke it in natural language

Objective: start a grill-me session on a topic of your own.

Grill me about .
One question at a time, with your recommended answer,
and checkpoint to a file after each response.

How to verify: it should create a file at brainstorms/, say where you saved it and do the first question — without dumping several at once.

🏢 Grill the business/process

The most valuable use: extracting a your business process from beginning to end. It’s the kind of thing that lives only in the mind of the person doing the work — and when documented, becomes a reusable skill, training, or context.

Example to run— complete discovery prompt

Objective: map out an entire process, with flags for anything you don't know.

Quero que você me grile relentlessly sobre o processo de
, do começo ao fim.

Regras:
- Uma pergunta por vez, com a sua resposta recomendada.
- Resolva dependências em ordem (decisão de cima primeiro).
- Se algo estiver num doc/código, leia em vez de perguntar.
- O que eu não souber, registre como flag com o dono certo
  (ex.: " -> ") e siga.
- Faça checkpoint em brainstorms/ a cada resposta.
- No fim, releia tudo, reconcilie contradições e me dê um
  recap: capturado, flags em aberto, próximo passo.

How to verify: by the end, there must be a brainstorms/AAAA-MM-DD-embalagem.md with Summary, Q&A log, and Open flags — and the recap must list at least one flag with an owner.

🎯 Tip

Always mark the variable parts with <colchetes> before pasting. The more specific the scope (“packaging process", not "my business"), the question tree is better.

Clear scope

A process, not “everything.”

Rules in the prompt

Serial, flags, checkpoint.

Variables

Marked in <brackets>.

Recap at the end

Captured · flags · next.

3

😰 The difficulty of answering

Here's the little-spoken truth: being grilled is uncomfortable. The questions touch on exactly what you've never put into words, and answering is tiring. That friction isn't a flaw in the process—it's the process working.

💡 Why discomfort is the work

The question that makes you hesitate is the one that matters most: it found something you knew how to do but had never explained. If nothing felt uncomfortable, grill-me wouldn’t be extracting anything new—it would just be confirming the obvious.

✗ The reaction that undermines you

  • ✗"That’s enough, it’s good enough" at the first sign of fatigue.
  • ✗Give a shallow answer just to get past the question.
  • ✗Treating the hard question as a nuisance instead of gold.

✓ The approach that extracts

  • ✓Treat discomfort as a sign that you've found something.
  • ✓Let the session run — it could be 5 questions or 30.
  • ✓Answer with the real nuance, even if it takes longer.

⏳ Set expectations

A good session can take more than an hour. That’s normal and good. The sign you should keep going is precisely the feeling of “I’ve already explained this” — there’s almost always nuance missing there.

Friction = value

The hard question is gold.

Don’t stop early

"Enough" is deceptive.

Duration varies

5 to 30+ questions.

Real nuance

Worth the wait.

4

🤷 When You Don’t Know

At some point, grill-me will hit a point where you don’t explain as well as the operator who does that every day. It’s not a failure — it’s information. It becomes a flag for the right stakeholder, and you get the answer later.

Example to run— how to answer when you don’t know

Objective: turn “I don’t know” into an actionable flag without blocking the session.

I don’t know this step in detail—the person who knows it best is the . Record it as a flag with that owner and
move on to the next question. I'll bring her response
afterward, and we'll update this brainstorm.

How to verify: the section Open flags the capture file gets an item <passo do estoque> → <operador> and the interview continues instead of getting stuck.

🔁 The “I don’t know” cycle

  • 1. The question touches on a gap in your knowledge.
  • 2. It becomes a flag for the person who knows (operator, finance, legal…).
  • 3. You find the person and collect the answer.
  • 4. Re-grill the brainstorm with the new information (next topic).
Gap = data

Show where you’re not an expert.

Flag → owner

Operator/stakeholder.

Search and return

It brings the answer later.

Honesty

Don’t make up what you don’t know.

🔄 Grill again

How each session leaves a document, you can return to it when you discover something new. The brainstorm is alive: found a process improvement, solved a flag, changed your mind? Grill again and update it.

Example to run— resume an existing brainstorm

Objective: update an old brainstorm with new information and resolve flags.

Leia brainstorms/<2026-06-22-embalagem.md>. Me grila de
novo só sobre o que mudou:  e a flag "" foi
resolvida — o valor é . Atualize o arquivo (corrija as
entradas afetadas) e me diga o que ficou diferente.

How to verify: the same file is updated (not a new one), the flag comes from Open flags, and the Summary reflects the change.

📌 Living doc, not frozen

In the reference video, that’s exactly what happens with the packaging brainstorm: when you find a “major breakthrough,” you go back to the doc and say, “here’s what I found, let’s update all of this.”

📦 From brainstorm to deliverable

Capture is raw material, not the final product. The raw always stays in brainstorms/; when the session produces a polished deliverable—a plan, a map, a spec, a skill—that artifact can move to the corresponding project folder.

1

Raw stays in brainstorms/

The raw interview log—Q&A, flags, synthesis—lives in a predictable place and never gets scattered.

2

Polished becomes a deliverable

From the raw material, you distill a clean plan/spec/skill—and that one does go in the folder projects/ of the work.

3

The return path remains

If someone asks "where did this decision come from?", the original brainstorm is still there and traceable.

Raw

Always in brainstorms/.

Polished

Move to projects/.

Separation

Raw ≠ deliverable.

Traceable

The origin is recorded.

⚙️ Grill-me + skill-creator

This is where the cycle closes. At the end of a session, grill-me realizes you have a skill and a doc with nuance that isn’t in them — and offers to update both. Combined with a skill-creator, is what takes a skill from ~70% to ~95%.

The nuance grill-me extracts doesn’t stay in the brainstorm: it feeds into updates to the skill and the doc. That’s “sharpening the axe” turning into a real cut.

🔗 The closing offer

In the end, grill-me says something like: "I noticed you have this guide and this skill, and there’s a lot of nuance here that isn’t in them — do you want me to update both?". You answer "yes"—and your OS gets smarter.

8

⛔ Anti-patterns

To wrap up, the mistakes that drain a grill-me session. Each one destroys one of the safeguards you learned in the previous tracks.

✗ Anti-patterns

  • ✗Answer in batches — kills the serial rhythm.
  • ✗Let skip the checkpoint — risks losing everything.
  • ✗Ask what you can explore no code/doc.
  • ✗Stopping too early at the first "it's good enough."

✓ The right path

  • ✓One question, one answer, one checkpoint.
  • ✓Require a checkpoint after every response in the prompt.
  • ✓Let the grill-me read before asking.
  • ✓Follow through until every branch is covered—flags resolve the rest.

⚠️ The worst of the four

Skipping the checkpoint is the most dangerous thing: in a long session, if the context fills up or the session crashes, everything that hasn’t been saved some. That's why the rule is non-negotiable—explicitly ask for the checkpoint in your prompt.

No batching

Always serial.

Don't skip checkpoints

Save every response.

Explore

Before asking.

Don’t stop early

Cover every branch.
